## Post 1

Any idiot can build an AI agent. Getting one to do useful work is the hard part.

It's become fashionable to talk about AI agents. People brag about how their "agentic workforce" works autonomously, never sleeps, and never asks for raises. Being a curious person, I decided to build some AI agents with Claude and see for myself.
I built a LinkedIn posting "team" that includes:

> a topic research agent,
> a writing/drafting agent,
> and an administrator agent that helps organize a Google Sheet of all the candidate topics and drafts.

None of the words here were written by my agents. Maybe you don't believe that because who believes anything is real anymore?

It only took a few hours to get my agents up and running.The problem was that the results were mostly useless. They chose uninteresting topics. The drafts weren't what I would have said, etc.

Many of the "runs" failed due to technical issues the agents should have been able to figure out on their own but didn't Refusing to give up, I spent many more hours fine-tuning agent instructions by hand-editing Markdown files to tell my agents:

a) what to do when they experience problems,
b) how many times to retry before aborting,
c) how to write to log files when they aborted,
d) what topics to include or ignore,
e) how to sound more like me,
f) what phrases to avoid because that's not how I talk,
g) dozens of other things.

It was a hassle. I have decades of software engineering experience, and this felt a lot like software engineering, even though I didn't write a single line of code. After spending about 30 hours, I finally had my agent team working better.

It's good enough to save me some time researching and rough-drafting LinkedIn posts, but it will never be something that can substitute for me and I will always be tweaking the agent instruction files to handle new issues or update context in a dynamic world.

The moral of the story: Be skeptical of those who brag about AI agents. It's mostly people wanting you to think how AI-savvy they are. Getting agents to do things like you would do them is painful.

Agents need constant refinement and maintenance.It's definitely possible to build AI agents that do useful work and save you lots of time. You can download or buy off-the-shelf agents to do very mechanical things really well. However, getting agents to approach human quality standards and basic common sense is much more difficult than our "agent fans" would want you to believe.

## Post 3

Anyone want to guess how far this bird travelled during migration? Weighing only half an ounce, it flew nearly 7,000 miles round-trip. We know this because new technology is revolutionizing our ability to gather data on bird migration. 

Researchers strapped an incredibly light "backpack" the size of a tic tac mint onto this yellow-rumped warbler's back. The device records atmospheric pressure along the flight path, storing data on board so it can be retrieved later. 

Amazingly, the pressure readings act as fingerprints for the bird's location so we can track the entire flight path. You can read more about it here: https://lnkd.in/g83Kypun

Researchers can now identify key stopover and wintering sites, so we can better understand what areas need to be protected. 
Human-caused destruction of stopover points where birds rest and refuel along the way makes migration difficult or even impossible. 

Birds need to migrate to survive. It's not optional. Technology definitely has its downsides for nature, but it's a double-edged sword that can also be incredibly helpful.

## Post 5

There’s a lot to criticize about AI. But we should also be willing to recognize when it’s being used for something genuinely good.

Microsoft’s "AI for Good Lab" highlights what a powerful conservation tool AI can be. One project funded by that lab uses AI to monitor biodiversity continuously in real time. Powered by solar energy, it operates autonomously in remote forests to gather and process visual and audio data on everything that moves or makes a sound.

Processing millions of images and sound recordings, it uses machine learning to filter out all the useless data and focus on what scientists need to know. It eliminates thousands of hours of tedious human analysis and lets us know what resources are needed to keep ecosystems healthy, without guessing. It's like an ecosystem EKG.

There's a lot of fear, uncertainty, and doubt over AI. Some of that is well deserved. But let's try to separate the good from the bad. We need to recognize companies that put some effort into leveraging AI for good purposes. Let's call out companies that are just "green washing", or not sincere, but when corporate initiatives make a difference, I'll recognize that without being cynical.

Here's more info about the AI for Good Lab's biodiversity project: https://lnkd.in/gnyYz2_3
